The pattern at the holy well

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

The veneration of holy wells, each one placed under the care of a particular patron saint, reached back into the earlier religion of the land and was carried into the Christian era with the importance of wells for baptism. On the feastday of the patron saint, the people of the neighbourhood gathered at the local well to pray, and the pattern — the Irish patrún, the pattern day, from the word for patron — was a great occasion. Around the prayers gathered music and dancing and drinking; the well became the heart of the whole parish for the day. Many cures were sought and told of the wells, and it was commonly said that a supplicant given a cure was said to see a fish appear in the water as the mark that a cure was to follow.

The well kept its guardians and its tokens. A special tree grew beside most wells, and pilgrims fastened a rag to the standing tree, or pushed a coin into the bark, as a mark left in reverence of the well and a thanks given to the well and its patron. The pattern day bound the calendar to the landscape: each well kept its own feastday, and the parish year was told out in the round of the wells and their patterns. Traditions even said that a well that was desecrated by some individual might go dry and then reappear at a new place nearby — the sacred site itself unwilling, in the telling, to remain where the injury had been done.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • The pattern is an Irish yearly/social praxis: the well's feastday gathers the whole community to pray, sing, dance and keep the well's patron.

  • The tree at the well took the pilgrim's rag or coin as a token — a mark of veneration left to stand through the year.

  • Praxis: keep the pattern of one's local well — come to it on its patron's day, make the round or the prayer, and leave the small token of rag or coin on the tree, first asking for nothing more than a leaving of thanks at the boundary of the year.

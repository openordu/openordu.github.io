The wren at Christmas and St Stephen's Day

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

In the old Irish countryside the wren was treated as a sneaking, unlucky little bird, held in dislike by birds and people alike since it was said to have deceived the eagle and made itself king of the birds. Because of this it was not disturbed in the ordinary run of the year; but around Christmas the old stories of the wren's treachery would be brought out — how the wren betrayed St Stephen hiding from his enemies, or how it gave warning of an Irish ambush to Cromwell's men.

So it was once the custom to gather on Christmas afternoon, and again on St Stephen's Day, to join in hunting the wren. Boys and men would form a band and go out with a small wren or a wren in a bush, carrying it from house to house, and the wren-bush of holly that was brought in at Christmastide was kept as a green badge of the day. The hunting had largely died out by recent times, and people kept the wren un-molested through the remainder of the year, but the clustered stories and the wren-bush showed how much the little bird, and the days that carried it about, nettled the imagination of the housefolk.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • St Stephen's Day (26 December) and Christmas Day both carry the wren custom; the bird is left in peace all the rest of the year.

  • The wren-house and the wren-bush bracket the two days, and the hunt is a piece of the old yule/Christmastide ringing of the village.

  • Praxis: on the day of the wren, carry the small bird round (now, in kindness, a wren-bush or a wren effigy) from house to house as a ringing of the dead of winter, and keep the green bough at the door.

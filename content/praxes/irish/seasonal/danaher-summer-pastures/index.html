The summer pastures — the booley year

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

At the opening of the mild half of the year the Irish cattle-farmers moved their milch-cows up to the mountain and moor grazing that stood worthless through winter but sweet in summer. This transhumance, the booley year, let a small home farm keep far more cattle than its lowland fields could carry, because the hay ground below was left unpastured to feed the herd in the cold months.

The move was planned in spring. The rough huts on the high ground were repaired or rebuilt before the cattle were driven up for May Day, and the herd stayed on the mountain pasture until the end of October. In some districts the shelters were dug partly into a south-facing bank, a single room with a hearth and a roof of sods or thatch. Whoever kept the herd lived up there all season, sometimes the young, sometimes the old. They milked the cows night and morning, churned the butter on the mountain, and the men of the home farm came up from time to time to carry the firkins away to market or to the town.

The life of the booley was often bright. The young women who kept the herds — and these tended to be two or three young sisters kept together — took dogs for protection and company, and on Sunday evenings the girls of the neighbouring buaile came together while the young men walked up the hill to them, so the high pastures rang with music, dancing and song. The cattle that passed the summer above were thought to mend in health and thrive.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • The booley is a seasonal Irish rhythm, not a fixed feast: the herd rose in early May and came down at the approach of winter.

  • Keep the older African-Atlantic pairing: the same summer-grazing system survived in the Pyrenees and the Alps and in Norway and Sweden.

  • Praxis: to honour the seasonal cycle of the pastures, mark the driving up of cattle (or of any herd or flock) to high grazing at the opening of summer and their return at the end, as a turning of the tending year.

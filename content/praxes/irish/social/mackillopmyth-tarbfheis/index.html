The tarbfheis — the bull-feast of kingship

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

The bull stood at the heart of the cattle-raising Celtic year, and the beast was turned to the heaviest of ends — the choosing of a king. The best-known rite was the tarbfheis, the divinatory bull-feast of the kingship, kept at Tara in Ireland. In that ritual a new king was not chosen by the candidate himself but by a man who feasted upon a slaughtered bull, lay down under a druid's incantation, and in the dream knew who was to be the new king. The bull carried the divination: the flesh of the slain beast, the sleep of the seer, and the dream of kingship came together in the one act.

In Gaelic Scotland a comparable rite, the taghairm, answered a person's questions about the future in a like manner. He who sought the answer wrapped himself in the fresh hide of a bull that had but recently been killed, still warm, in a lonely place and preferably near a waterfall, and so entered a trance in which the answer was told him. The two rites are the practical edge of a wider reverence of the bull — carved bulls guard the old places from Burghead in Scotland to Maiden Castle in Dorset, and the great bulls Donn Cuailnge and Finnbennach carry the fortunes of their provinces in the Táin.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • The tarbfheis at Tara turned the slaughter of a bull into the means of a king's finding — the lot of the beast read as the lot of the realm.

  • The taghairm of Gaelic Scotland is the same rite turned to a private question asked of the future.

  • Praxis: treat the bull as a bringer of omen and of kingship in the old cattle-year — not by the slaughter in our own day, but by the keeping of the herd's strength and the reading of its health as a sign of the fortune of house and land.

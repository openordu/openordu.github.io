Nuada's Day

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

Nuada's Day honours Nuada Airgetlám, Nuada of the Silver Hand, the first king of the Tuatha Dé Danann and the type of the lawful, right-ruling king in the Irish tradition. The name remembers the arm he lost in battle and the hand of silver made for him, the emblem of a kingship that was maimed yet restored, broken yet made whole. The historical sources assign him no fixed seasonal liturgy; the commemoration at the opening of November is a reconstructed observance that sets him beside Samhain as the cycle turns to the rule of law and council through the dark months.

For the devout polytheist, Nuada's Day is a feast of right order: a day to honour the one who lost sovereignty that his people might keep their claims to the land, and who passed the kingship on when his wound returned, setting the precedent that a blemished or unjust ruler must give way to the whole. It is kept not as a holiday of heroes but as an observance of the king's justice that holds a community together — the silver hand as the hand of the lawgiver, remembering that kingship in the Irish tales is covenant with the land and its people.

The devout keep the day by the hearth and the bargaining-table rather than the altar alone: by reading the older stories of the Tuatha Dé, by confirming one's own word and settling what is owed, by honouring those who bear honest authority and by recalling that power held rightly is power held in trust. It is a day to set one's own house in right order as the winter courts sit.

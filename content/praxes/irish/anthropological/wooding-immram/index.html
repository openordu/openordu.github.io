The immram — the voyage of many islands

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

The immram is the Irish voyage-tale: a sea-voyage in which a company of voyagers — often a cleric and his followers, or the hermit and the saint — is carried across the ocean from island to island, meeting at each a wonder, a trial, or a stage of the otherworld. The earliest and best-known, the Immram Brain, tells of Bran mac Febail sailing west to the land of women and the otherworld islands; the Navigatio Sancti Brendani, in a Latin dress, gives the Christian saint the same course through the wonders of the sea. The voyages are counted among the immrama, the rowing-tales, named for the rowing that carries them.

Scholars read in the tales a recurring shape: the sea-voyage serves as a framing device, a leitmotif that carries the voyager through a succession of island-encounters. In the otherworld-adventure (the echtrae) and in the exile-tales (the loinges) the same voyage appears, sometimes as the whole point of the story and sometimes as the mere vehicle. What the tradition holds in common is the sea as the boundary: the voyage is the crossing into the might of the otherworld, where the voyager is tested, changed and, in the saint-tales, restored to a landed and blessed return. The whole literature keeps the one image — a small vessel, a rowed or sailed passage, an island-chain — as the figure of the soul's wide adventurous journey.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • The immram and the echtrae are the voyage genre of early Irish literature, used as both matter and figure of the crossing.

  • It is a liminal, boundary-crossing praxis, nearer to the anthropological turning of the year than to any fixed calendar day.

  • Praxis: take time in the year for a crossing of one's own — a retreat to an island of the mind, a day-long journey to an inward or outward shore — made as a voyage of many places (enter going-out, change, return), the older figure of the soul's sea-way.

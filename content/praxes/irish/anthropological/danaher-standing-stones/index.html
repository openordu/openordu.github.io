The standing stones — gallán as grave-marker

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

The tall stone pillars that stand singly over the Irish countryside were once raised as grave-markers. The great Punchestown long stone, a pillar held upright as a National Monument, overlooked a grave site at its foot; the excavators found evidence that the stone had been set to mark where a person of weight lay buried in the deep past. The same holds for the group of pillars within a few miles around it — Furness, Harristown, Longstone Rath, Craddockstown and others — where burials were found at the foot of more than one of the pillars. The giant cairns and passage mounds such as Newgrange and the Knocknarea cairn were piled up with several thousand tonnes of stone, the work of many hands, and even a single pillar like the one at Dol in Brittany, standing thirty feet and more high, is a serious feat of engineering — one that the stone-raisers may have handled with much the same tackle as the shear-legs and winch used to re-erect the fallen Punchestown stone in modern times.

Yet not every éct stone was ancient, and not all were memorials. A Kerry farmer jested that the gallán the antiquarians gave to the Bronze Age was, by his own and Uncle Jerry's testimony, only a scratching-post for the cattle. The recorded associations are with burial and with place: the stone stands as a turning-mark in a field, a token of the dead, a weight of memory on the land.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • The gallán is the standing pillar; the long stone at Punchestown stands as one of the biggest pillars of its sort in the country.

  • The stones are place-markers and grave-markers, read both as monuments of the dead and as landmarks of a neighbourhood.

  • Praxis: regard the standing stone as a boundary of remembrance — a place to pause, to name the ancestors held in the mound beneath, and to keep the memory of the land through the working year.

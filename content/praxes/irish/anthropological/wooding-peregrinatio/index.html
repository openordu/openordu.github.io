The peregrinatio — exile as a sacred crossing

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

Among the early Irish, the peregrinatio — the exile or pilgrimage that set a person forth on the sea in search of God — became a recognised religious path. The wanderer for whom the exile was a calling turned his back upon homeland and kin, went down to the sea, and gave himself to a voyage that was at once a penance, a search and an offering. The image of the Irish as a people of pilgrims and exiles was fixed in the mind of European Christendom; Walahfrid Strabo, the ninth-century scholar, wrote of exile in foreign lands as an evocation of the wandering that was the Irish mark, and medieval Europe found the vision attractive.

The voyage was not merely an end but a practice with its own shape. The immram and the voyage tales tell of going down to the great bounds of the ocean, of voyages that ended at island after island, of the sea as the crossing-place of the seeker. The peregrinatio is counted among the harsher of the ascetic ways: to leave all that one knew, to sail without fixed port, to dwell in the loneliness of the sea fast — these the early Irish held as a whole-hearted giving of oneself to the boundary-crossing where the otherworld may reach the voyager.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • The peregrinatio is the founder of the voyage tradition: an exile that is at once a penance, a pilgrimage and a search.

  • Reached its full shape as one of the ways a person turned wholly to the seeking; wanderers, hermits and saint-voyagers all walked the same road of the sea.

  • Praxis: give weight in the killing of the year to the acts of return and of leaving — a journey taken in the old mind as a crossing, a going-out, and a turning-back which is not the same place that was left. The sea-crossing is the old type: an exile for a purpose, taken willingly.

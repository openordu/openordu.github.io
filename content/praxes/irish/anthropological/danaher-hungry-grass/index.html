The hungry grass — the marbh-luachra on the path

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

In lonely country, especially on the mountainis, the old people spoke of dangerous patches of earth and grass where a wayfarer might unwittingly step and be struck by a sudden, overwhelming hunger. The belief was widely held: there were patches of hungry grass on the mountainsides where a traveller who walked over them was afflicted with a great hunger that could, in the worst telling, bring death. To guard against it, country people carried a little bread or food in the pocket when they went on a journey, and eating the bread would turn aside or obviate the magical hunger.

The power of a lonely place reached further than the belly. A "magic fog" was believed to settle round a person in such lonely spots, so that he or she went on straying and lost, unable to find a way out until daylight came and released them from the ordeal. Comparable accounts in medieval literature tell how heroes found themselves wrapped in such a fog, standing at the threshold of an otherworld adventure. The bread in the pocket, like the wayside verses against the phantoms, was a small armoury against the beings and places that could take hold of a person who walked alone at night through the kind of places where the otherworld reaches.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • The hungry grass is a threshold-ward: a protection carried for the crossing of wild, lonely places where the boundary with the otherworld was unshielded.

  • Married to the wayfarer's world: the walking people who lived on the roads knew best the danger and the remedy.

  • Praxis: walk the lonelier ways with bread carried free, in the old manner, and be courteous when the road turns strange; the small offerings keep the hunger and the wandering fog at hand only in the older boundaries.

The butter charms — guarding the churn

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

Butter was not mere table garnish in the old Irish farm economy but a principal source of income, sold at the market fair by weight and quality. Because so much turned on it, the making of the butter was hedged about with careful custom and with a sharp fear of harm done by spell. A farmer whose churning repeatedly failed or whose butter came tainted drew dark suspicions of butter-stealing — the belief that a neighbour, often an old woman who worked as a hare, had taken the cream's power by magic so that it ran to her own churn instead.

The customs of protection turned on the churn itself. Anyone who came into the kitchen while the churning was under way had to lay a hand on the churn-dash or its handle, so that having shared in the work one could not steal its fruit. Nothing might be lent from the house during churning — no drink of water, no coal for a pipe, no begging — for the giver would in effect be giving the good of the butter away. The milk, the cream and the butter stood open to harm from the milking until the firkin went to market.

Charms and counter-charms joined the everyday care. A red coal from the hearth was dipped in the churn, or a piece of iron heated and used the same way; salt preserved against evil magic; twigs of rowan or elder hung in the byre; a silver coin was laid on the cow's back at milking. When harm was suspected the wise woman, the bean feasa, was called, and one of the remedies put a hot plough-share into the churn so that the un-doer of the charm was forced to own the wrong and give back the stolen butter.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • A weekly rhythm: the churning and the market day framed the small producer's week, and the butter was carried in coopered firkins to the town for sale.

  • The protective customs are protections of the working household: they guard milk, cream, butter and cow, and they bind the whole house into the work of the churn.

  • Praxis: tend the dairy by the older ways — guard the churning from hindrance, keep the byre with rowan or elder, let the blessing of the house lie on the milk as it is worked, and know who it is that lends nothing forth at churning time.

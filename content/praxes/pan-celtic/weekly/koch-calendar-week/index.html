The seven-day week

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

The seven-day week did not grow up inside the Celtic world; it reached it through contact with Rome and the church. Yet once it arrived it took root in every Celtic tongue, and the Brythonic languages — Welsh, Breton, Cornish — are distinctive in keeping the Latin weekday names almost unchanged. The Celtic week is therefore a Roman inheritance worn with a local accent, and its names carry the history of that borrowing.

In Welsh and Breton the old solar and planetary day names survive: Sunday is dydd Sul or ar sul, from the Latin "day of the sun"; Monday follows the moon, dydd Llun and al lun; each other day keeps its planetary name after Mars, Mercury, Jove, and Venus. The Goidelic names are a different cloth: Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday among the Irish and Scottish take their shape from aín, the Old Irish word for a fast, so Wednesday is literally the "first fast," Thursday "between two fasts," and Friday the "last fast" or simply aín. Where the Brythonic trace the wandering planets, the Goidelic trace the church's weekly fasts.

One habit worth recovering is inclusive counting. The Welsh word for a week really says "eight nights" and the word for a fortnight "fifteen nights," because the Celts counted both ends of a span. The Old Irish sechtmain, a loan from the late Latin word for a week, is the one Goidelic term that does not keep this frame, but the Brythonic habit survives in speech.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • Keep the seven-day rhythm, and let the naming teach a double lesson: the planet days in a Brythonic land, the fast days in a Goidelic one.

  • On Friday, the "fast" day of the old week-name system, observe a light and uncluttered keeping — the name itself is a reminder.

  • Count each span with both ends joined, as the old night-span words do, when reckoning a week or a fortnight of devotion.

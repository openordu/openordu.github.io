Beltane (Bealtaine, May Day)

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

Beltane ranked among the four great seasonal feasts of the Celtic year, held on May first at the opening of summer. It was an agricultural festival tied to the cycle of grazing and planting. Its central act was the bringing of cattle out of the sheltered winter paddocks and up to the summer booleys (buaile), where the grass was fresh.

Weather divination mattered at this feast. Frost was read as an omen of a poor season to come, while rain was read as a sign of plenty and a strong harvest.

The festival opened in the dusk of the eve, as with the other great fires. On Beltane the veil between the visible world and the unseen realm was thought to thin enough for spirits and the dead to visit the living. Where Samhain was the festival of the dead, Beltane belonged to the living, whose vibrant spirits came forth seeking union with the daylight world.

Fire stood at the heart of the feast, and the name runs through words for fire. Hearth fires were put out, then rekindled from a great bonfire lit on the nearest signal-hill. In Ireland the hills were said to burn in answer to the sacred fire of Ériu at Uisneach or at the royal centre of Tara. At Beltane the herds were driven near or between the fires to ensure their safety for the coming year, and it was held especially significant if a white [beast] passed through. The fires continued to be lit in Wales into 1840 and in Ireland through the middle of the twentieth century.

The Glen Lyon valley in the Scottish Highlands keeps a survival of this fire rite around the beginning of summer on May 1.

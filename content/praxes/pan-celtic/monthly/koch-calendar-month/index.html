The months of the Celtic year

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

The month names used across the Celtic lands are a mirror of how each people watched the turning year. Latin reached far in, so most month names are plain borrowings, but beneath the surface the older observing habits show through — months that point at the crops, the beasts, and the light.

September the Welsh call Medi, the harvest; the Breton word for the same month is bound up with ripened grain and white straw. November carries words for the dark and the slaughter — the Breton du and kerzu for black, the Welsh Tachwedd for the butchering month, a near cousin of the old English "blood-month." December is the black month in Scottish Gaelic and Breton alike, and its Welsh name Rhagfyr speaks of the shortening day.

The Irish and Scottish months keep two of the great feasts in their very words. The name of August still registers Lugnasad, the festival of harvest begun at the start of that month, and the name of November holds Samhain in its shape. October in Scotland, Dàmhair, keeps a word for the deer's month. One Scottish word for January, Faoilleach, is bound to a festival word and can mean either the first fortnight of February or a carnival-time near the turn of the year, and it may be kin to the word for welcome or for the wolf.

For the devotee the names are a quiet liturgy: each month spoken aloud carries its own weather and its own feast. The harvest sits in the word for September or August, the dark in the word for November and December, and the four great festivals ride inside the very names of the months that hold them.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • Speak the month's own name where a local one survives, and let it set the tone: harvesting, slaughtering, black cold, or lengthening light.

  • Let the feast-months (August and November, the touch of Lugnasa and Samhain) be trimmed, before the festival itself begins.

  • Read the Welsh and Breton names as a seasonal clock: "harvest" is the signal to gather, "slaughter" and "black" the signal to close in.

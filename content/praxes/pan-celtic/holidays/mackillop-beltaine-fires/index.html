Beltaine fires — the kindled turning of the year

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

Beltaine, the first of May, closed the dark stretch of the year and opened the light, one of the four great festival hinges of the Celtic calendar. In early Ireland the day was known as Cétshamain, and it carried a matching set of names across the islands and the mainland: Cyntefin and Calan Mai in Wales, Cala' Me in Cornwall, and Kala-Hanv in Brittany. It was held a lucky time to open a great work — the Book of Invasions tells that the Partholonians and the Milesians alike landed in Ireland on Beltaine.

The fire was the heart of the feast. Huge bonfires rose in Ireland and across the Isle of Man, and in Scotland they might burn on the mountain-tops; the flame could be raised as teeth éigin, the need-fire or fire struck from wood. Hearth fires were put out and rekindled from the new fire as an act of purification. A central requirement of the day was that the herders pass their cattle through the gap between two fires to guard them against contagion through the coming year. Those who danced about the fire did so sunwise, to the right, and on Man they carried rowan twigs or branches. Effigies of witches were cast into the Scottish fires even in the later centuries.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • The four great festival hinges of the old Celtic year are Imbolc (1 Feb), Beltaine (1 May), Lughnasa (1 Aug) and Samain (1 Nov); Beltaine and Samain frame the hinge of the year.

  • Protect the herd between two flames; rekindle the household fire from the new-kindled flame; walk the turning sunwise.

  • Praxis: at the first of May, raise a hearth-fire or a small bonfire, pass the beasts (or the household's good) between or through the smoke as a keeping of the year, and turn the house to the light of the coming summer.

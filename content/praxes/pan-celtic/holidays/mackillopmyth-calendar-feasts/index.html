The four fire-feasts and the reckoning of time

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

The old Celtic way of telling the year rested on the four great feasts that were set on the opening days of the months that come second, fifth, eighth and eleventh months — Imbolc on the first of February, Beltaine on the first of May, Lughnasa on the first of August, and Samain on the first of November. These were the four day-markers of the year, harmonising the seasons with the moon and with the working of the cattle-year: MacKillop notes that the Celts measured time by vegetation, by the breeding of stock, and above all by the seasonal driving of cattle to the high grazing, the transhumance that turned the year.

Samain, the autumn feast that the Christian calendar pushed back to the last day of October, was the hinge of the whole reckoning. The Gaulish bronze tablets of Coligny, with their sixty-two months and their months marked MAT and ANM as good or ill, show that the year began with Samonios, autumn's start, because the dark precedes the light. The Celtic year did not number itself: it turned in a circle, each year much like the last.

The reconstructionist caution belongs in the same note. The feast that modern invention calls by the old name is not the modern mischief that the new age has made of it. What the historical record shows is a decorous, agricultural, kinfolk calendar — four hinges to the year, and neither the wheel-with-eight spokes nor the theatre of the solstices. The solstice-sites of Newgrange and Gavrinis were hewn long before the Celts came, and the modern gatherings at Stonehenge are innovations no older than the eighteenth century.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • The four feasts of the historical Celtic calendar are Imbolc, Beltaine, Lughnasa and Samain, on the first of their months; the year begins with Samain in the winter quarter because the dark part of the year comes before the light.

  • This entry deliberately parts from the seasonal holiday-wheels of later invention, which rest on no historical Celtic ground — the real Celtic year is the four-feast agricultural and cattle calendar, with no other day counted a festival-night.

  • Praxis: keep the four fire-feasts of the year and let those four hinges carry the whole turning of the house; do not look for a wider circle of festival-nights in the later holiday-wheel.

The quarter days and the four feasts

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

The Celtic year ran on four great hinge-points, and those hinges set the seasonal rhythm of the devout polytheist. In Ireland the four festival days are Lá Fhéile Bríde on the first of February, a continuation of the old feast of Imbolc; Lá Bealtaine on the first of May, heir to Beltaine; Lá Lúnasa, Lammas on the first of August, heir to Lugnasad; and Lá Samhain on the first of November, heir to Samain. Each is more ancient than the Christian name that now carries it, and each stands a full quarter of the year away from its fellows.

These four are not the only quarter-days. The English half-yearly terms fall on Lady Day, Midsummer, Michaelmas, and Christmas; the Scottish on Candlemas, Whitsuntide, Lammas, and Martinmas. But it is the Irish four that descend most directly from the old festival calendar, and it is they that anchor the praxis matrix: Imbolc at the lambing, Beltaine at the fire of summer's coming, Lugnasa at the harvest, and Samhain at the threshold of the dark half.

A caution attends the fixed dates. The Gregorian reform was taken up at different times in different Celtic lands — France and Brittany in the 1580s, Scotland in 1600, England and her territories in 1752, the Isle of Man in 1753 — so for a century the English and Scottish calendars ran ten days apart. Some regions kept on marking feast-days by the "old calendar," and customs have drifted between nearby feasts. The devotee should not chase an exact astronomical fit; the four quarter-days are a repeating frame for holy work, kept on the fixed first days of the month.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • Anchor the year on the four Irish festival-days: 1 February, 1 May, 1 August, 1 November.

  • Keep them as fixed feasts, not wandering observances; the old calendar and the new settled on these dates.

  • Follow the sequence faithfully: lambing and the fires of Brigit, the fire of Beltaine, the harvest of Lugnasa, and the dark threshold of Samhain.

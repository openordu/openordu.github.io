The Beltaine bannocks — the lot of the black spot

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

Beltaine was a season for the ritual tasting of certain foods, and the best-known among these was the Beltaine cake or bannock. The bannock was a large, round and flat bread, usually baked from oats or barley, made in a single great sheet that could be broken into a number of portions. One of the pieces was marked with a black spot, made by sticking charcoal into the dough.

The portions were shared out, and the one who by lot drew the piece that carried the black spot became the Beltaine carline, or the old hag of the day. That unlucky winner might be subjected to a mimed punishment — a mock execution in which he or she was cast into the fire, or a feigned drawing-and-quartering. What survives clearly is the shape of the custom: a communal meal, a portion divided among the gathered, a black-marked share drawn by chance, and the chosen one made to bear the role of the old hag as the fire's scapegoat for the year ahead.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • The bannock-baking tied the whole gathering into one loaf that was then split by lot, making the feast a single shared act.

  • The black-spot share is a scapegoat rite: one bears the burden so the rest are cleared.

  • Praxis: bake a large bannock of the old grain, break it among the gathered in a real drawing of lots, and let the one who draws the marked share be gamed and honoured rather than harmed — the humours of the day turn on the lot, not on anyone's destiny against their will.

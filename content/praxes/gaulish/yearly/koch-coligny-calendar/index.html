The Coligny calendar

Praxis Jump to heading ¶

In 1897, near the village of Coligny in the Burgundian country of eastern France, diggers turned up the broken fragments of a great bronze tablet — the most complete record we have of how the continental Celts kept time. Now held in Lyon, the surviving pieces preserve nearly half of the original, a lunar calendar whose bronze the Gauls cast in the shape of the inscribed timekeeping tablets of the classical world but measured by the moon where those measured by the sun.

The Coligny calendar runs on a five-year span of twelve lunar months, each of twenty-nine or thirty days, with two intercalary months slipped in over the five years to pull the lunar count back into step with the sun. Each month is marked MAT or ANM — auspicious or inauspicious — guiding whether the days within were fit for undertaking. The month named Samonios carries the same root as Irish samain, the word for summer's end and the great November feast; the month Giamonios, half a year away, holds the root for winter. If Samonios falls at the start of the year, then the festival of Samhain begins the Celtic year itself.

Four quarter-days are pricked out on the bronze, seated in four of the months; if the calendar turns on Samhain these fall at the equinoxes and solstices, though whether they were kept as festivals or only used to wrench the year back to the sun is not recorded. One festival the bronze does name directly: trinox samoni sindiu, "today is the three-night Samhain," wording that marks a rite kept across three full days and nights. A fragment from Guidizzolo in Italy sets the feast of the horse goddess Epona at mid-December, a dating some have tied to the winter solstice and to the Welsh Mari Lwyd and kindred customs of the dark midwinter.

Culture notes Jump to heading ¶

  • Honour the moon's count alongside the sun's: the great festivals were first fixed by a lunar frame, not a solar one.

  • Reckon a festival by nights, not days, in the manner of the three-night Samhain of the bronze.

  • Let the seasons turn, but fix the fires by moon and by the four quarter-days of the old calendar's turning year.

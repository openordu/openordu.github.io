    Oppida

    Christopher Godwin 2026-08-27 05:26:47 about 3 minutes

    region period term place group Oppida La Tène Switzerland Gaul central Europe Belgae Camulodunum Colchester Kelheim La Tène D Pfostenschlitzmauer Rhine

    Oppida is an archaeological term. This Latin word describes fortified Celtic towns. The word once meant a town central to its territory and people. From Caesar's time, it named large defended tribal centres. These proto-urban settlements spread through Gaul and central Europe, reaching southern Britain. Caesar set the oppidum against the aedificium, a building, and the vicus, a village. Greek writers called the same places a polis or phourion. La Tène in Switzerland is a famous example. Today Celtic scholars use the term for the large fortified settlements of the closing La Tène phase. Some call that phase La Tène D. Builders placed oppida beside watercourses. Rivers and marshes formed part of the defences. Ditches and ramparts sealed any gaps. Ramparts often grew to enclose the whole settlement. Enclosed areas ran about ten times larger than in earlier periods. They spanned 0.2 to 15 square kilometres. Camulodunum at Colchester enclosed 31 square kilometres. Builders made ramparts from wooden beams and earth with an outer stone facing. One form, the Pfostenschlitzmauer, used inset posts. These walls with inset posts appear often east of the Rhine, with Kelheim as the type site. The older settlements of the sixth and fifth centuries B.C.E. stood on heights or promontories. They often lay near mineral deposits that supplied the settlement's wealth.

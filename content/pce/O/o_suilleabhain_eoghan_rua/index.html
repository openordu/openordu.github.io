    o suilleabhain eoghan rua

    Christopher Godwin 2026-08-27 05:20:28 about 8 minutes

    Institution Place Person o suilleabhain eoghan rua Meentogues Killarney Kerry Munster Admiral Rodney the British Navy the Caribbean

    O Suilleabhain Eoghan Rua, a Munster poet who lived from 1748 until 1784, was remembered in the local tradition for his sharp wit and his restless manner of living. He was born at Meentogues, a place east of Killarney in County Kerry, and for most of his years he earned his keep partly as a schoolteacher taken on for short terms and partly as a labourer who moved about working in different districts of Munster.

    One story says he was a great admirer of women and that, to escape a difficult involvement, he joined the British Navy some time in 1776. A second account, probably more reliable, holds that the press-gang seized him, and it is the version he himself tells in verse. The year 1782 found him in the midst of a fierce sea-fight between British and French ships in the Caribbean, where he wrote a piece in English heaping praise on the victorious commander Admiral Rodney. He appears to have hoped this tribute would win his discharge, but Rodney refused him. He then pestered the authorities without let-up, using spearwort to raise blisters on his shins, and the sores grew so bad that at last he was sent away from the service. He came home to Ireland, but not long afterwards he was killed in a tavern brawl that a satire he had written set off. His verse moved within familiar topics, yet it was wonderfully rich in language and metre, and this red-haired, carefree young fellow gained a name as a wonder at words. Like the other Gaelic poets of his age he knew the older Irish literature intimately and took the best schooling the hedge-schools of the time could offer. He handled English well and possessed some Latin, some Greek, and possibly a little Hebrew, which the common folk simplified into a belief that he had command of seven languages and could summon poetry and history at will. He put his fluency at the service of ordinary people, turning his lines against meanness, self-importance and harsh authority, the three faults the public most despised. A tale shows him sowing seed for a cruel, unmoving farmer who would not give him his dinner and bade him carry on. He obeyed, but matched his labour with a verse curse, praying that the drill in the field might hold neither luck nor fortune, that the crop would not thicken, that ruin and scattering would fall on the man's children, and that a thunderbolt would smite the house. The farmer, dreading the spell, begged him to come and eat. Why verse came so readily to some and so rarely to others puzzled the folk, so they told of a plain fellow who asked the poet the secret. The poet replied that it was only speech, and, walking along, strung four careless remarks of the man into a smooth rhyming set, proving that rhyme could flow as easily as talk. Yet he could also wrap himself in mystery. Once, famished, he slipped into a wedding feast but was placed with the tramps and given nothing but sprats. Taking up a sprat he feigned a whispered talk with it. The host saw him and asked what he was at. He answered that he was questioning the sprat about his brother's dealings abroad; the host, who had a brother overseas too, asked him to enquire further, and after more muttering the poet said the sprat knew nothing but had sent him to the hake, and the hake had pointed to the cattle, for the man in question was said to be herding abroad. Thus he was at last seated at the beef table, where he ate greedily and told them the host's brother abroad was faring most handsomely. This is an old trickster tale retold to fit the poet. Whether his famous love-affairs rested on fact is uncertain, but folklore delighted in them and showed him as a roguish, likable gallant. They began early: at the age of seven, late for school and facing a thrashing, he recited a set of polished verses about two pretty women he had come upon travelling, and the schoolmaster, charmed, forgave him. In later life a small boy who answered everything in rhyme was greeted by him as his own son, a pattern found in many lands. In yet another telling, a woman shoved her weeping child at him and demanded that he mind it, whereupon he made up a lullaby promising the baby every lovely thing a poet could conceive. A much-loved set of stories sets him against overbearing churchmen. In one, a priest announced from the altar that he meant to rebuke the poet for his loose ways and called out twice to know whether he was present, first by his full name and then by his nickname; a reply came from among the congregation that the poet hoped the priest would never set eyes on a painter, a joke upon Rua, the term meaning red, for a painter needs red on his palette. Another time, mending a torn shoe at the roadside, he was mocked by a stout priest who said he was no great cobbler, and he capped the taunt with a verse about a priest whose head was full of Latin and whose belly was lively with drink. When he was refused admission to Mass for having no offering at the door and told to remain outside, he seated himself upon a pile of turf and answered, in a rolling measure, that it was not his poverty that stung him but the insult to his pride, and that if heaven was as rigid as its ministers he did no worse for sitting on the turf; the priest relented. He offered one day to ferry a priest over a swollen river upon his own back, provided the man heard his confession along the way, and mid-stream he confessed a fearful sin; the enraged priest cried that the devil had mounted his back, to which came the reply that the devil would not ride long, and he pitched the man headlong into the stream. He is also shown chiding a slothful clergyman who would not get up one blustery evening to visit a sick woman, telling him she would not want for spiritual company along the way to heaven. These anticlerical tales mark a folk demand for charity and a hostility to rank, and other verses rebuke the clergy for greed and indifference to the poor, a protest with a long history in Europe. The picture of Eoghan Rua has the full force of the sinner-saint image, and it agrees with the poems he truly wrote; his life joins a squalid daily struggle to the soaring reach of his imagination. Two traditions surround his death, both probably invented. One says that after he was dealt a blow upon the crown with a fire tong in the deadly quarrel and lay helpless for a while, a young woman, wondering if he were truly dying, undressed by his bed; he answered with a frank verse that his strength was spent, and she understood that his end was close. The other reports that the Bishop of Kerry, hearing of his death, mourned that the loss grieved him more than the passing of a hundred priests, for a priest could be made wherever money could be found but a poet like O Suilleabhain Eoghan Rua could never be made again. These sayings echo tales told of famous painters of Renaissance Europe, and they round off the story of a man who belonged at once to his own people and to the wandering spirit of Munster.

    • o suilleabhain eoghan rua: Munster poet remembered in local lore for his wit and unsettled lifestyle; born 1748 at Meentogues east of Killarney in County Kerry; worked as a part-time teacher and wandering labourer in various parts of Munster; died 1784 in an ale-house brawl produced by a lampoon he had composed.

    • o suilleabhain eoghan rua: tradition speaks of his womanising and of his entering or being press-ganged into the British Navy about 1776; in 1782 in the Caribbean he witnessed the British-French sea battle and wrote an English poem praising Admiral Rodney to win his freedom, then was discharged only after blistering his shins with spearwort.

    • o suilleabhain eoghan rua: his conventional but linguistically and metrically rich poetry made this red-haired rakish man a popular verbal wizard; folk stories and verses satirise stinginess, pomposity and oppressive authority, with flourishing anecdotes of the farmer's curse, the trickster sprat, the seven-year-old poet, the son met in verse, the clergy clashes and a lullaby.

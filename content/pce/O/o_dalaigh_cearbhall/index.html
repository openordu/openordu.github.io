    o dalaigh cearbhall

    O Dalaigh Cearbhall is a famous romantic poet in the Irish folk tradition. The surname O Dalaigh, written O'Daly in English, marked a family whose craft was the making of verse. Different branches of the house served the chief lords of Ireland as their poets. The name Cearbhall was much loved among them. The most celebrated poet of that name is truly a single figure shaped from several different historical men. The family traced its line to Aonghus O Dalaigh, a poet of the thirteenth century who lived in Westmeath and had seven sons. One son, Cearbhall Fionn, whose name means fair-haired, was ancestor of the branch that settled in County Cork and wrote for the MacCarthy family. That Cork branch devised a legend about their forefather that served as the mark of their trade. The poets knew the tale of how the king of Cashel won his throne by an unlucky deed, and the tale of how Fionn came by wisdom and poetry through chance. From these stories, and from the old image of poetry as the milk of a fairy cow, the Munster O'Dalys fashioned a story of their ancestor Cearbhall Fionn. The story passed into the folk memory of Munster and of Connacht and is retold yet. The Munster account sets O Dalaigh a little boy in the service of a farmer, whose work was to mind the cattle. Each evening the farmer asked whether he had seen aught strange, and the boy answered that he had not. One day he watched a cloud settle on a bed of rushes and a dun cow eat them. When he told his master, the man told him to bring the earliest milk of that cow back to the house. As he took it, a drop touched his finger, and he tasted it first. The change was immediate. His face shone and his speech was verse. The farmer knew the boy had taken the drink meant for himself and drove him away. From that time Cearbhall was a wandering poet. Because he had first tasted the milk, he kept the art of verse and many powers of magic as well. The Connacht form of the tale says a black bull stood within the cloud, came to the cow and gave strength to her beastings, though this is a later addition. The family mark arose in the fourteenth or early fifteenth century. Toward the end of that time a romantic text was made in Connacht with a Cearbhall as its hero. In it, the daughter of the Scottish king saw the young man in a vision and fell to loving. He crossed to Scotland with a company of poets so they could meet, and there he put the court asleep with his music, saving only the two of them, to speak with her. The king suspected the lovers and threw the poet into prison, but the princess visited him, changed clothes with him, and freed him. Deceived by wizards, he forgot his love and wed another, but the princess came by magic to the naming feast and inked her very name in blood onto his harp. The sign brought back his memory, and his bride left. At last the Scottish king paid some Irish men to tell the princess that her lover died, and she died of a broken heart. The men told Cearbhall of her death, and he laid his head upon his harp and died. The writer placed his hero in northern Ireland and dated him to 1404, but plainly mixed up many men. Another famous bearer of the name dwelt in the north of Wexford early in the seventeenth century, and certain old lines remain in which he and a friend asked one another whether he truly was that Cearbhall of earlier days. He loved, it is said, the daughter of a Kavanagh lord, and he left two poems for her. A story, known all over Ireland since the eighteenth century, tells how Cearbhall married a young woman but, at the feast, heard of a fairer lady. He made show of being a weakling, so the marriage was not kept, and the fairer lady was truly his love. He took work with a shoemaker, and when she had him measure her for shoes, they fell in love. Her father had chosen a richer suitor and would not allow the match. On the night of the betrothal, Cearbhall came to the house as a beggar and proved himself a fine singer. He played music that set the company to sleep and carried her away, turning his horse's shoes so none could trace. In one ending the two were caught, and the poet by his wit showed he was a fit husband. In another they died in a distant land, for he fell on a spear in his trick and she sickened of grief. The sad ending ties the story to the older romance. A song of the sixteenth century carried a name close to that of the lady, and found its way into the tales. In Munster the sign of love was brought into the story, and the poets said Cearbhall wore the love spot and opened his coat that the lady might see it and love him. The figure of the bard, for all the mock and the wonder, grew ever greater in the telling.

    • o dalaigh cearbhall: is a celebrated romantic poet of Irish folk tradition, a composite of several historical bards of the name

    • cearbhall fionn: was the fair-haired ancestor of the Cork O'Daly poets who served the MacCarthys

    • fearbhlaidh: was the daughter of the king of Scotland who loved the poet in a dream and died of a broken heart

    • eleanor kavanagh: was the beloved of the Wexford Cearbhall, daughter of Sir Morgan Kavanagh, with whom he eloped

    • goban saor: was the mythical craftsman whose lore colours some of the poet's tales

    • donn: was the otherworld lord whom the poet met at the entrance of a cave beyond Knockfierna

    dearg

    Christopher Godwin 2026-08-28 08:44:05 about 5 minutes

    Person Deity dearg Da Dearga Conaire Donn Fionn mac Cumhaill Derg Corra Daire Daghdha Cernunnos

    The name Dearg, which preserves the older form Derg, is borne by a mythic figure whose place lies deep in the ancient lore of Ireland. On its own the word signifies little more than ‘red’, yet that plain hue carried an ominous weight, for the being was closely connected in the mind of the tradition with slaughter and the approach of death.

    No single, settled personality is affixed to him in the records that survive. Scholars have long noticed that by the literary age the name, along with its character, had been shaped into a narrative device that an author could seize upon to fill a tale with dread. The being known as Da Dearga supplies a striking case, for it was within the hostel of Da Dearga that the figure of Conaire came to his bloody end. In the text itself Da Dearga is addressed by the shorter title Derg, and the opening element ‘da’ is read as a shortened form of ‘dia’, the Old Irish word for god. His godly nature is suggested once more by the three horsemen, each called ‘Derg’, who arrive to presage Conaire’s death and who ride the horses of Donn, and by the matching phrases ‘the house of Derg’ and ‘the house of Donn’. Out of these signs the natural judgment forms itself: the figure of Dearg stands in some way for the same being as the far better-known death-god Donn. He may have been merely a further title for Donn wherever a violent fate impended, or, to judge by the course of the Conaire story, the name that the early men of Leinster most often used for Donn.

    Within the tradition of Fionn mac Cumhaill, the celebrated hero of Leinster, the figure appears as a hostile presence from the other world, a notion that probably takes rise in a fanciful contrast drawn between Donn and the prototype known as Find, from whom Fionn descends. Much like ‘Donn’, the name ‘Dearg’ occurs in several passages as an epithet beside the name Daire, who likewise seems to be a presiding partner of the Daghdha. The whole fellowship of such ancestral figures was, it is held, carried into the setting of Find throughout the long centuries before Irish letters began. One odd offshoot is a curious narrative in an eighth-century writing, telling how Fionn mac Cumhaill kept a serving-man known as Derg Corra. A lover of the hero pressed her attentions on this servant, and when he spurned her she charged him falsely with attempting to rape her. Trusting that claim, Fionn banished the man, yet later, while hunting, he came across him in the woodland. Derg Corra sat there eating with a blackbird, a trout and a stag. A cloak of concealment disguised his identity, but he was at last recognised through the magical wisdom of Fionn. The plot was taken over from an outside source, noted as Romantic Tales and its type numbered 870C, though the imagery set upon it is quite distinctive.

    That imagery answers to Celtic engravings from several parts of western Europe in which a human figure stands with a stag and other beasts. One surviving carving keeps the inscription Cernunnos, a term read as ‘the horned, or peaked, one’, and the same reading fits the name Derg Corra, giving the sense ‘the red peaked one’. From this it is said that lingering relics of ancient devoted belief became bound to the divine title Dearg. Portraits of a sacred deer would naturally prove attractive to the maturing legend of Fionn mac Cumhaill, whose stories made much of deer-hunting. The bond between Dearg and the wild deer lasted within that branch of tradition, and the mediaeval narrators brought his daughter to Fionn shaped as a doe (see Oisin).

    • dearg: Dearg (earliest Derg) is a mythic personage of ancient Irish lore, his name literally meaning red and his figure being linked with sinister ideas of slaughter and death; the surviving accounts grant him no single identity.

    • dearg: The Conaire tale in the hostel of Da Dearga, where da shortens dia meaning god, the three Derg horsemen riding the horses of Donn, and the parallel of the house of Derg with the house of Donn all argue that Dearg is in some way equal to the death-god Donn.

    • dearg: In the Fionn mac Cumhaill tradition he is a hostile otherworld figure tied to the Donn/Find contrast; he is an epithet with Daire, a double of the Daghdha; the 8th-century story of his servant Derg Corra with the blackbird, trout and stag, the Cernunnos horned one image, and the daughter coming as a doe.

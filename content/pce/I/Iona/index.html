    Iona

    Christopher Godwin 2026-08-28 11:36:08 about 5 minutes

    Concept Group Artifact Person Place Iona Saint Columcille Colum Cille Inner Hebrides Eilean Ì Island of Mull Sound of Iona Ioua holy well Tuatha Dé Danann Danu Sheela Na Gig yew Blathmac Picts Uí Néill Iona Chronicle Book of Kells Easter controversy Whitby Kells Adomnán Iona Community Church of Scotland Viking

    Iona is a Scottish mythological site. It is now best known as the center of Celtic Christianity founded by Saint Columcille. The tiny island sits in Scotland's Inner Hebrides. A Celtic shrine to a goddess once stood there. The goddess Ioua gave the island its first name. She has been read as a moon divinity. Others connect the name to a word for yew, a tree of sacred places and death. The island's holy well rose from the magic cauldron of the Tuatha Dé Danann. Its name, Tobar na h'oige, means fountain of youth. Those who looked into the well at dusk, at dawn, or on the night of the full moon saw visions and received healing. As a Celtic site it admitted both men and women. Under Christianity women, except nuns, could not live there. One of the island's churches keeps a figure called a Sheela Na Gig. She is a pregnant woman shown exposing herself.

    Gaelic speakers also call the island Eilean Ì, or simply Ì. The Sound of Iona separates it from the Island of Mull. The island spans only about 12 square kilometres. Its position sits between Ireland and Gaelic Scotland. That central spot made it a religious and cultural focus in the Middle Ages. In 563 the Irish monk Colum Cille, also called Columba, founded a monastery there. He belonged to the Northern Uí Néill royal line and died in 597. The monastery became pivotal to the Celtic church. Iona helped Christianise the Picts in the later sixth century. It drove the second conversion of Anglo-Saxon Northumbria in 635. The monk Blathmac was martyred on Iona. He refused to reveal the location of Colum Cille's shrine. The abbey's scribes kept one of the earliest written records in Britain. That record, the Iona Chronicle, spans about 686 to 740. Later Irish annals rest on it. Seventh-century scholars fuelled the Easter controversy over the date of Easter. Iona lost that dispute, so its influence declined after the 664 meeting at Whitby. The Book of Kells, an illuminated gospel, probably began at Iona in the late eighth century. Kings of Scotland, many of them buried on the island, held Iona in high veneration. The Iona Community rebuilt the ancient buildings in the mid-20th century. The Church of Scotland consecrated the abbey in 1959. The project now serves all Christian traditions as an ecumenical centre.

    • Ioua: a moon divinity whose name the island originally bore

    • holy well: rose from the cauldron of the Tuatha Dé Danann, its name meaning fountain of youth, giving visions and healing

    • Colum Cille: an Irish saint of the Northern Uí Néill lineage who founded the monastery on Iona in 563 and died in 597

    • Eilean Ì: the Gaelic name of the island, also called simply Ì

    • Island of Mull: the neighbouring island from which the Sound of Iona separates Iona

    • Sound of Iona: the strait that separates Iona from the Island of Mull

    • Iona Chronicle: an early record kept at Iona from about 686 to 740 that became the basis for the early Irish annals

    • Blathmac: a monk martyred on Iona for refusing to reveal the location of Colum Cille's shrine

    • Picts: a people Iona helped to Christianize in the later sixth century

    • Book of Kells: an illuminated gospel book likely begun at Iona in the late eighth century

    • Easter controversy: a calendar dispute fuelled by seventh-century Iona scholars that Iona ultimately lost

    • Iona Community: a Christian group that rebuilt the island's old buildings in the mid-20th century

    • Church of Scotland: the body that consecrated the Iona abbey in 1959

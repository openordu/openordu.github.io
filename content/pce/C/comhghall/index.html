    comhghall

    Christopher Godwin 2026-08-27 05:20:28 about 6 minutes

    Family Place Person Comhghall Colm Cille Columbanus St Fionntan Ciaran of Clonmacnoise Brude Fiachna mac Baodain the Dal nAraidhe Bangor (Beannchair) Iona Bobbio

    Comhghall, known to posterity as a holy founder and reckoned to have lived roughly from the year five hundred and fifteen until the year six hundred and two, was a churchman of great standing in early Ireland. He is best remembered for establishing the celebrated monastery at Bangor in County Down, the establishment anciently known as Beannchair, while the name itself is commonly turned into the English form Congall. In his origins he came from the people known as the Dal Araidhe, a kindred that held lands across the southern part of Antrim and the northern reaches of Down. He was on close terms with the great saint Colum Cille, and at one stage he passed some time alongside that figure while both were engaged on the expedition to Scotland. His standing as an educator was considerable, since among those he discipled was the celebrated missionary named Columbanus, who lived approximately between five hundred and forty and six hundred and fifteen and bore the Christian message across much of the European mainland, establishing in the process the well-known religious house at Bobbio on Italian soil. Even granted the personal pride that Comhghall commanded, the two accounts of his life that have come down to us seem to have been composed no earlier than the twelfth century. Those writings are set down in Latin, though they lean upon much older matter that was very likely composed in Irish. Tradition relates that the priest who sprinkled the water over the infant Comhghall was without sight, and that the man recovered his eyes only through a spring that welled up at the place to yield water, finding a detail almost literally lifted from the older legend of Patrick. When Comhghall was still young the country fell into conflict and he was called to stand in for his aged father, who was beyond the age for bearing arms. He was permitted to quit the campaign when snow came down upon the camp and, as the story goes, fell everywhere about him yet without touching him at all. Choosing instead the religious calling, he traveled to the cloister known as Clonenagh in the county of Laois, whose acting superior was the holy man Fionntan, and afterwards paid a visit to Ciaran at the famous foundation of Clonmacnoise. Once back in Ulster he received the solemn orders and went on to oversee the beginnings of the house at Bangor. Many acts of healing and even of restoring the lifeless are told of him, and it is said that his prayer cell shone with a marvellous light whenever he withdrew there, a token of his singular holiness. There is also the old tale that when the grain ran short in a season of scarcity, a wealthy man refused to part with any of his abundant stores to feed the brothers, and Comhghall warned him that the mice would finish off all of his corn, a prediction which duly came to pass. Other accounts in the same spirit narrate how the saint brought various overbearing lords and lawless men to their proper place. At another moment he crossed to the settlement of Colm Cille upon the isle of Iona and, together with Colm and with Cainneach, he set out to win over the Pictish ruler Brude. When the party arrived outside Brude's stronghold Comhghall caused the gate to be cast open, Colm threw wide the doors, and Cainneach made the king's hand shrink and shrivel when it brandished a sword against them. Once the king had accepted the faith, his hand was fully restored to him. It was also told that after Comhghall had come back to Bangor a monk from foreign parts journeyed to the monastery hoping to outdo him in strict works. Comhghall brought the visitor down to a river at midnight and bade the man stand with him there to chant the psalm in that place. As the two lingered in the water, the stream kept shifting between blazing heat and bitter cold, and in the end the newcomer owned himself beaten. On another occasion Comhghall brought back the queen Caointighearn, the consort of the king of the Dal nAraidhe, from a state of grave sickness after one of her serving women had slipped her a draught of poison. The handmaid repented of the deed, was forgiven by the saint and thus escaped all punishment. His memory lived on in the naming and dedication of churches in Ireland as in the Scottish lands, and the day set apart to recollect him is the tenth day of May. Taken together, all these stories colour the picture of a ruler of churchmen whose legend was carried by the folk memory far beyond the single Irish home it came from.

    • comhghall: saint who founded the monastery of Beannchair (Bangor, Co Down), lived c515-602 AD, his name anglicised as Congall

    • comhghall: belonged to the sept called the Dal nAraidhe and was a friend of Colm Cille, spending time with him on the mission to Scotland

    • Comhghall: was teacher of the missionary Columbanus, who carried Christianity across continental Europe and founded the monastery of Bobbio

    • St Fionntan: was abbot of the monastery of Cluain Eidhneach (Clonenagh, Co Laois) when Comhghall went there to take up the religious life

    • Brude: king of the Picts whom Comhghall, Colm Cille and Cainneach set out to convert

    • Fiachna Mac Baodain: Dal nAraidhe king whose wife Caointighearn was poisoned by a handmaid and restored to health by Comhghall

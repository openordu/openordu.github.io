    conghal clairingneach

    Conghal Clairingneach is a fictional king of Ireland, placed in the country's prehistory. His name is taken to mean 'warrior-valour', while his sobriquet is rendered 'flat-nailed'. Though several tales of him circulated in mediaeval literature, no dependable early account has come down to us. There exists, however, a lengthy text dating from about the fifteenth century that recasts episodes already familiar to the literature for some centuries before it. That account synchronises Conghal with the heroes of the Ulster Cycle and so draws his reign forward to the closing years of the first century before Christ. This text, composed in east Ulster, recounts how the high king Lughaidh Luaighne cleft the province of Ulster in two, setting Conghal Clairingneach over the one share, with Fearghus mac Léide given the second when they divided the spoil. The leading men insisted that their province come under a single sovereign, and the poet Fachtna Fionn conducted both rulers before Lughaidh at Tara so that they might ask this of him. There his daughter Fionnabhair grew infatuated with Fearghus and talked her father into granting the Ulster kingship to him. By way of recompense Conghal was offered lands in each province, yet he rejected the terms and stalked away from Tara in fury. He presented himself to Cairbre Crom, king of Meath, who urged him to repay the insult, and various banished princes gathered to his side. As he made his way back into Ulster he came upon Cnomhthann Caomh, Lughaidh Luaighne's own son, beside the Boyne and put him to death. When the tidings reached Lughaidh he grieved deeply, yet he bestowed Fionnabhair upon Fearghus mac Léide as his wife, and the man pledged to return to Ulster and drive Conghal out. Fearghus then kept a lavish banquet at Eamhain Mhacha, the capital of Ulster, where his namesake Fearghus mac Rosa, that is Fearghus mac Roich, was among the guests. Strike broke out among the men of the two rivals, and when mac Rosa began to doubt that a military tribute owed him by mac Léide would be honoured, he drew his forces away from Eamhain and threw in his lot with Conghal. Conghal dispatched the poets Fachtna Fionn and Bricne, otherwise Bricriu, to Eamhain to summon his friends and kinsmen, but Bricne started to menace these men and they would not come over. Acting on the counsel of his tutor, Conghal turned aside from war against his fellow Ulstermen, yet he permitted Fearghus mac Rosa to assault and raze the stronghold that Niall Niamhghlonnach held at Dun Da Bheann, the mount known as Sandel beside Coleraine, in retribution for the slights he had suffered at the feast. Niall answered by marching out against the combined army, and the two forces clashed at Aonach Inbhir Thuaighe, where the Bann meets the sea; in the bitter fray Niall was cut down and his host scattered. Fachtna Fionn now advised Conghal to sail overseas with his company, and so they did. After a number of adventures in Scotland and beyond they came back to Ulster, where Fearghus mac Rosa rejoined them, and together they made for the hostel of Boirche in the south-east of County Down, at which Fearghus mac Léide was then lodging. Fearghus mac Léide and his force sallied forth against them but were routed, whereupon Conghal resolved to attack Tara, dispatching Fachtna ahead to challenge Lughaidh Luaighne, who begged a single night's respite to assemble his troops. This was granted him, yet in the battle of the following day he came up against a Conghal in full fury and was beheaded, his Luaighne sept cut to pieces. Conghal then assumed the kingship of the whole island, and Fearghus mac Léide made his way to Tara to give himself up; Conghal spared him and conferred the throne of Ulster on a warrior by the name of Ros Ruadh. The passage describing Conghal's doings abroad is plainly a late mediaeval invention, one that may well have been composed by the writer of the text himself. It records that Conghal's hand was pledged to Taise Thaoibhgheal, a daughter of king Donn, ruler of the other-world, whom the story lodges on Rathlin Island. Nabgodon, styled king of Uardha, the 'cold country', yearned for Taise. While Conghal and his warriors were feasting with Donn at his hostel, Nabgodon fell upon them with a mighty host, and in the savage struggle Conghal beheaded him while every attacker perished. Long were Conghal and his men in recovering from their wounds, and during that time envoys carried word to Fearghus mac Léide, and he granted a tract of land to Taise and even offered the kingship of Ulster to Conghal. Yet Conghal chose instead to sail for Scandinavia, where he and his men were magnificently entertained by that realm's king, Amhlaoibh. The mischief-making Bricne went to the monarch's fair daughter Beiuda and whispered that Conghal was enamoured of her. She answered that he must fetch her three marvellous birds, a great chariot-yoke and a heavy helm. All of these lay in a faraway sea-fortress commanded by the amazon Muirn and a great band, yet after many deeds of combat Conghal and his companions won them. On his return to Scandinavia he declined to wed Beiuda, since she had cast him into such peril. He then crossed into Scotland, taking several islands and accepting the submission of the British and Saxon kings before turning homeward to Ireland. Conghal's end was fitted to the close of his great royal struggle, for it is said that he journeyed into Munster to exact hostages because of the help that province had once given Lughaidh Luaighne. The Munstermen refused him the hostages, and in the battle that followed they were beaten, while Lughaidh's son Cairbre was wounded and walked lame ever after. This Cairbre had two sons, Duach, also called Dui, and Deadhadh. The brothers quarrelled, and Duach had Deadhadh blinded, earning the name Duach Dallta Deadhaidh, that is, Duach who blinded Deadhadh. It was this Duach who struck Conghal down, and he then mounted the throne of his grandfather, though no particulars of that slaying endure.

    • conghal clairingneach: fictional king of Ireland set in prehistory; his name is interpreted as 'warrior-valour' and his sobriquet as 'flat-nailed'

    • conghal clairingneach: given one half of Ulster by Lughaidh Luaighne alongside Fearghus mac Léide, who won the whole province through the favour of Fionnabhair, daughter of Lughaidh Luaighne

    • conghal clairingneach: after the poet Fachtna Fionn advised it, he went overseas on adventures in Scotland and Scandinavia, facing king Donn, Nabgodon, Amhlaoibh, Beiuda and the amazon Muirn before winning the kingship of Ireland at Tara

    • Duach Dallta Deadhaidh: the son of Cairbre, grandson of Lughaidh Luaighne, who blinded his brother Deadhadh and later slew Conghal Clairingneach to mount his grandfather's throne

    • Fearghus mac Léide: ruler placed over half of Ulster, rival of Conghal, who surrendered at Tara and thereafter held no banishment

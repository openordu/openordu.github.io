    cromwell oliver

    Christopher Godwin 2026-08-27 05:26:47 about 6 minutes

    Person Place Event deity Cromwell Oliver Maoilsheachlainn Mac Amhlaoibh Beelzebub Duhallow Ireland the devil the battle of Worcester Merlin

    Cromwell Oliver commanded the parliament's forces during the English civil war and in the end ruled as Lord Protector of the Commonwealth and of Ireland as well as of England. His invasion of Ireland, which lasted some nine months from the summer of sixteen forty-nine, cut deep into the memory of the country, and the saying 'the curse of Cromwell' (scrios Chromail) came to stand for the most bitter kind of malediction. He is remembered largely for the severity of that war, yet the stories that circulated about him in Ireland dwell mostly on small personal meetings with him that bear little relation to his real soldiering. Much of this narrative plainly borrows the abuse that the royal faction in England pressed against him in his own day, and the people of Ireland, with their own hurts from him, were ready to believe every word of it.It had been charged by the English royal party that a woven image of the evil one decorated the birth-chamber, and Irish retellings transferred this to his drill-field, claiming he had a painting of the devil set on the parade-ground gate, and that the figure bowed whenever Cromwell walked past. The drawings of his adversaries habitually placed him beside the devil, and one Irish story in that vein has a cleric who met Cromwell on horseback and respectfully hailed both riders, then learned to his awe that the second was the fixture riding at his back.It was observed in England that he died on the third of September sixteen fifty-eight, a full seven years, to the very day, after his crushing win at Worcester, and it came to be reported that he had pledged his immortal part to the devil in exchange for a septennium of victories. This report was fertile ground in Ireland, and many believed his war-making strength flowed from that dark bargain. The tale gained force from a wild storm that raged in southern England while he lay dying, which royal partisans took as the devil's arrival for the soul of his servant. That tempest is remembered yet, and accounts from the last century speak of a dreadful craft upon the sea, pulled by great black hounds while its grim master announced he had come to fetch Cromwell.A mocking verse composed upon his death, printed as a broadsheet, pictured the fallen ruler ferried over the Styx into hell, and a folk tale from Kerry repeats lines drawn from that same piece. In a stage show put on in Cork in sixteen eighty-five he appeared with a golden nose which the devil plucked as he dragged him away. The humor of an enormous nose began in English mockery, and it was kept alive in Ireland, where the jest turned his nose into one of copper or of brass. Yet certain features of his legend appear to have grown up in the country itself.One of these is the claim that he slipped into Ireland in secret to discover the character of its people and the way to defeat them. He came to a home where a corpse was being watched, and tried to coax the widow into slicing a piece off the dead man for money. She spurned the offer at first but bent when he raised the price. He also paid a man at the same wake to trip a drunk companion into the hearth fire, and, well pleased at such frailty, he judged that Ireland was already his, since its folk would sell and even roast one another for silver.A widely repeated tale, told in both Munster and Connacht, tells of his business with a prophet named Maoilsheachlainn Mac Amhlaoibh, the chief of Duhallow in County Cork. A chief of that title truly lived and did write foretelling verses about the country's fortune, but the account of his traffic with Cromwell is plainly a later invention. In that account his mother was taken into a fairy fort, lay with a being of the other world, and was set free again when she was with child. Her husband the chief never guessed the boy was not his, and the boy, by reason of that other-worldly begetting, grew up with the gift of foresight and came at length to hold his father's place. In a vision he saw a humble English shoemaker who would climb to great authority and in time command all Ireland, and that cobbler was named Cromwell.Maoilsheachlainn at once sought out the craftsman, and after a search found the fellow a pleasant sort amused at the grand fancies of the stranger, yet willing to write in his own blood a pledge never to dispossess the prophet. When Cromwell had risen to mastery and came through Duhallow to seize the land, he saw the blood-written bond, remembered his word, and passed the chief by. The two men talked of what lay ahead, and the prophet spoke to him smoothly of a happy future. Yet an evil end was also foretold, for when he stopped at a smithy to have his mount shod he grew impatient and pushed a musket into the fire to beat out a shoe, and a shot still lodged inside went off and wounded him.Other versions promise that he should live exactly as long as he might wish, and that he perished by slashing his own neck while shaving in a fury. A further telling has him swear his sway over Ireland would endure only while he remained there, and so he asked that his body rest in Irish earth. The land, however, spewed his corpse forth into the channel between the two shores, and the contest of the seas has kept those waters boiling ever since.This final tale is borrowed from the medieval life of the wizard Merlin, whose sayings were taken seriously in the political councils of England. Placing Cromwell in the part once played by an ancient tyrant of Britain, the story-tellers substituted an Irish seer whose Munster name, Maoilsheachain, sounded to local ears rather like Merlin, and so the chief of Duhallow stepped into the role of that old prophet.

    • cromwell oliver: was the English parliamentary leader and Lord Protector whose 1649 campaign in Ireland earned him the harshest imprecations in Irish speech

    • Maoilsheachlainn Mac Amhlaoibh: was the chieftain and seer of Duhallow whose covenant with Cromwell was a late folk growth

    • the devil: was said to ride behind Cromwell and to have bought his soul for seven years of success

    • Ireland: rejected his buried body and cast it into the sea

    fitzgerald gearoid iarla

    Group Person deity Place Fitzgerald Gearoid Iarla Maurice Aine Gofraidh Fionn O Dalaigh Ailill Olom Lough Gur Knockainey the Geraldines Emperor Frederick the Second

    Fitzgerald Gearoid Iarla was the third Earl of Desmond, and he stood at the head of the Munster line of the Geraldines, the leading Norman kindred of later medieval Ireland. In Irish speech the family name became Mac Gearailt. He inherited the earldom in 1358 and twice held the office of Chief Justiciar for the crown. He counts as one of the clearest cases of a Norman lord adopting Gaelic habits, and the chronicles say his wisdom was spoken of throughout the land. He wrote love verse in the Irish tongue and was known for it. His castle rose beside Lough Gur in County Limerick, in the middle of the Desmond territory, whose title comes from an Irish phrase meaning south Munster. Not far off stands Knockainey, the hill of Aine, a goddess who in earlier tradition watched over Munster kingship and whom an old text makes the partner of Ailill Olom, the legendary king of the Eoghanacht.The new Norman rulers of Munster were eager to claim such emblems for their own. Gofraidh Fionn O Dalaigh, a professional poet kept by the Geraldines in the fourteenth century, called Gerald's father Maurice, the first Earl, the king of Aine, and spoke of Gerald as the child of her knight. The same idea survived in the country tales of the Knockainey area, which say that Maurice was walking along the edge of Lough Gur one day when he came upon the radiant otherworld woman Aine at her bath. He caught up her cloak, and that seized hold of her by enchantment, and so Gearoid Iarla was begotten, and when the boy was born Aine carried him to the earl's castle to show him to his father. O'hogain points out that this narrative welds together two separate strands: the Gaelic belief that the king of Munster lay with Aine, and a story from the Continent that great families owned a swan-maiden among their forbears.A further Continental tale fastened on Gearoid Iarla was the legend of the swan-knight, well known in the French, German and English writing of the later Middle Ages, about a knight who comes in a boat together with a swan, weds a lady, yet goes back off with the bird once she breaks a prohibition by demanding to know who he is. In the Geraldine version the prohibition is laid by Aine on Maurice: he must show no wonder at anything their son does. When Gearoid is grown he proves his nimble magic by jumping into a bottle and out of it, and his father cannot stop himself from staring, and at once Gearoid quits the castle, wades into the Camogue stream nearby, and goes off swimming in the shape of a goose. Because an islet on Lough Gur bore the name Gearoid's Island, an Irish proverb that wild geese never come back to their old ground was taken to refer to this very affair.The best-known tale to cling to him, though, held that he had not perished at all but had slipped away from the world in some hidden manner, and that belief was tied to the fate of the Desmond house in the years after his end. Through the fifteenth century the English crown grew uneasy at the Desmond earls, who reigned over Munster almost as sovereigns, and the Gherardini, a leading Florentine house, professed kinship with them. At the opening of the sixteenth century hostile talk against the Geraldines was spread in England, and once a rising failed in 1534 the Kildare line was almost wiped out. In 1558 the fourteenth Earl of Desmond came into the title, another Gerald, the same name his line had last borne in the celebrated Iarla. The English stripped away his wide estates and kept him much of the time a prisoner in London, and when at length they let him return to Lough Gur in 1573 he sided openly with the Irish cause kinsman's revolt.Hopes ran high, and prophetic promises were passed about to back the Geraldine cause. Through their Florentine links the family would have known the story of the Emperor Frederick the Second, who died in 1250 but was said to be living still and waiting to come back, and signs appear that his legend mingled in Geraldine minds with the longing for their own fortunes to turn through a new Gearoid Iarla. So common report began to insist that the long-dead Gearoid Iarla had never truly died but had slipped away without trace from one of his castles, and waited to return at the moment of the family's deepest need. And, again like Frederick, he was counted a learned man given to magic, which gave birth to a particular story of how he went away.He was said to keep a hidden chamber at Lough Gur where he cast his spells. His wife became curious and begged to see the work, and he bade her keep silent no matter what met her eyes. He then put on one fearful shape after another, and at last one so dreadful that she cried out. The prohibition was broken, and the castle plunged to the bed of the lake, taking down Gearoid and his whole household, and so he was believed to lie under the water, under a spell until the hour came for him to rise and put everything to rights.The great prophetic tale itself reached further. Tellings of it occur throughout Europe for many famous men of history, but in Gerald's case it looks to be taken straight from Frederick. A man travelling past Lough Gur by night saw a light on the side of a hill, went up to it, and came on the mouth of a cave in which he found a host of knights and horses asleep, with a handsome sword lying on the ground. As he began to draw the blade the host started to stir, and its captain, Gearoid Iarla, called out loudly asking whether the hour had come, but the man panicked and ran off. The host fell back into sleep, and the opening of the cave could never be found afterwards.The Gerald who bore the name in living history was no able soldier, and by 1583 his fight was lost and he was killed by treachery. What followed was the ruin of Munster at the hands of Elizabeth's armies, so that one annalist wrote that from Dunquin to Cashel scarce a cow lowed or a ploughman's voice was heard. Yet the people's love of the Geraldine line held firm, along with the wish that one of them would set the country free, and allusions to this mystical faith turn up in writing and records from the seventeenth well into the nineteenth century. The tales of the enchantment and of the slumbering army are repeated still, and another telling has the spellbound earl ride round Lough Gur on midsummer night once in every seven years upon a white stallion shod with silver, and when those shoes are worn away he will come back. The same store of legend was told also of the earls of Kildare and of the O'Neills and O'Donnells of the north and of a chieftain named Donall O Donnchi who dwelt by the Lakes of Killarney, plainly taken in each case from the story of the Desmond earl, and Munster folk-belief drew other tales of sorcery and the otherworld about him besides.

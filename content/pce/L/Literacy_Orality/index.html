    Literacy and orality

    Christopher Godwin 2026-08-28 11:36:08 about 3 minutes

    concept Language Institution Literacy and orality Celtic languages Latin Celtiberian ogam the Church

    Literacy and orality shaped the sharing of knowledge in early Celtic societies together. A literate society still depends on writing, and yet a long stretch of its past predates the written word. Recorded history is brief when set against the immeasurable ages that came before. Many of the ancient Celtic languages possessed writing systems of their own. Celtiberian, Gaulish, Lepontic, and ogam were all used for Celtic speech. None of these systems survived into the Middle Ages. Those scripts arose within pre-Christian learning, which helps to explain why none persisted. A new written tradition suddenly appears in the early Christian period, set within the intellectual sphere of the church. Most early Celtic major literature and documents were written in Latin, while the Celtic vernaculars carried on without a comparable break. If writing fixes speech through graphic signs, then speech itself holds a largely unrecorded history that runs before and beside writing. Oral culture can live next to written culture and even be the very reason writing exists. The Greeks used an alphabet for centuries before spreading it widely, and the epics that reached them as texts began as celebrated oral poetry. Modern scholars compare those epics with the oral poets of the South Slavs. Within such coexistence, oral tradition can grow into written literature. Caesar reports that the Celtic druids kept their learning out of writing, even though they used Greek letters for ordinary accounts. They deliberately hid their esoteric material, making orality itself a conscious decision. Calling oral peoples 'barbarian' and literate ones 'civilized' reflects an ideology, not a neutral fact. Monastic and scribal literacy then carried the records forward into the early history of Wales.

    • Literacy and orality: coexisted in early Celtic societies, with writing and spoken tradition feeding each other

    • Celtic languages: had ancient scripts such as Celtiberian, Gaulish, and ogam that mostly failed to survive the medieval period

    • the Church: brought a new literate culture and written history into medieval Celtic settings

    • Gaulish druids: deliberately kept sacred learning out of writing while using Greek letters for ordinary records

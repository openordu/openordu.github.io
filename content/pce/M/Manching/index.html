    Manching

    [, , ', C, h, r, i, s, t, o, p, h, e, r, , G, o, d, w, i, n, ', , ] 2026-08-28 11:28:09 about 4 minutes

    Group Place Manching Ingolstadt Bavaria Munich Boii Bohemia Vindelici Danube Paar

    Manching numbers among the chief towns of the continental Isle. It rests near Ingolstadt in what is now Bavaria. A wide Celtic settlement once filled this ground. Excavators have dug one of the largest prehistoric towns of central Europe. The age of strong building runs to the end of the La Tene era, the final centuries before the common age. The site also stood as one of central places of the Celtic tribe called the Vindelici. So it was more than simple village; it was a royal press of a people.

    The town found its seat about a kilometer from the south bank of the Danube. It lay where the river Paar meets the stream. Looking down from above, a deep circular area of some 2.2 to 2.3 kilometers spanned the fields. A huge defence wall of nearly 7 kilometers long ringed the town. Workers built it in the ways of the murus gallicus, a wall of stones and timbers that lock. When that older craft went out of use, men rebuilt the line as a timber-framed wall of a different type. This is known by the name "pfostenschlitzmauer". The defence had probably four great gates. The point of the eastern gate is exposed in the dig.

    Two small brooks once cut the space inside. Men drew these streams to run before the outer edge of the wall. That act added water to the defence of the town. Some of the outside of the oppidan the diggers found more works. A small square sacred enclave, a viereckschanze, stood only a short way off. The clear smell of iron smelting came from several stations near the place. Around these public works the yearly life of the settlement moved.

    The diggings have made the wealth precise. The workers worked in two great seasons, 1955 to 1961 and again 1984 to 1987. These efforts set Manching among the most explored of all. Inside the wall the houses lay about, each held by its own fence. The diggers divide the town into at least three phases of growing. They date from the later La Tène, starting near 200 before the common, well into the first century of the new calendar. Odd parts of the land were left empty within the wall. These empty heights were only low and might be tilled as farmland. This tells of a town of both dwellings and fields.

    A written find ties the settlement to a known people. Diggers found a late La Tène jar in the town. The vase bore the four letters BOIOS. That group gives the Boii the "the man of the Boii". The find dates to the first age before the common. It links Manching to the Keltic tribe of the Boii. That same Boii people also gave their name to the far land of Bohemia. The jar is a quiet link in a chain of power. Today the handful art sits in the museums of Munich and Ingolstadt, with a smaller set kept in a house on the land. The old walls still tell the plainest story of a people and their pride.

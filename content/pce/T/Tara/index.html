    Tara

    Christopher Godwin 2026-08-28 11:36:08 about 4 minutes

    Person Artifact Group Place Tara County Meath Tuatha De Danan Hill of Tara Navan Dunshaughlin Mound of the Hostages Rath of the Synods Ráith na Ríg Lia Fáil Tailtiu Saint Patrick Loegaire mac Néill Diarmait mac Cerbaill

    Tara is a prehistoric hill site in County Meath, the land the Irish call Contae na Mí. The Irish once called Ireland itself Ériu. The hill's own name marks a great and lasting kingship. Many call this office the high-kingship of Ireland. Tara holds the seat of the ard rí na hÉrenn. The hill sits between the towns of Navan and Dunshaughlin. It is not a grand high rise. Its plain shape hides a long and deep past. The archaeology of the hill is hard to read. It does not stop at the monuments you can see. Digging and geophysical surveys keep finding more human life. The ground holds more than the eye meets. The main monuments form a clear set. There is a Neolithic passage tomb from the New Stone Age. A long linear earthwork runs across the hill. A hill-fort stands on the high ground. A standing stone rises on the ritual mound. Legend ties this stone to Lia Fáil, the crowning stone that cried out under a true king. That story may be false. The stone may have moved to Tailtiu long ago. Many mounds of unmarked age dot the hill. Their dates stay unclear. In the 1950s workers dug into three places. They opened the Mound of the Hostages, a passage tomb. They dug deep at the Rath of the Synods. They cleared the ramparts of Ráith na Ríg, the royal ring. A second dig there in 1997 found signs of Iron Age metalwork. The old Irish name of the hill is Temair. It comes from an older Celtic word. Scholars read that word as a height with a view. Some call it the gates to the Otherworld. Another reading is a sacred place. Many writers take this last sense as the best. Tara became the seat of Ireland's high kings. The old tales give these kings a high place. Their rule spread over the whole island. Gods such as Lug, Medb, and Eithne enter these stories. Yet the hill was not their home. It was the seat of human kings. Those kings stood ready to guard the hill against the unseen. The old capital stayed alive in the books. The best loved tale is the story of Saint Patrick. He lit the paschal fire on Slane in the plain of Brega. The old story brings King Loegaire mac Néill face to face with him. Loegaire ruled as king from Tara. His druids stood close at hand. The earliest forms of this tale date to the seventh century. The writers Tírechán and Muirchú set it down in that age. Legend tells that a curse fell on the hill. A holy man spoke against its king. That king was Diarmait mac Cerbaill, dead in the year 565. The curse is said to have left the hill unused. He was the last king to hold the older wild rite. So the great hill fell silent after a long public life.

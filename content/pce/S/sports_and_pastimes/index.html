    sports and pastimes

    Christopher Godwin 2026-08-27 05:20:28 about 12 minutes

    occupation topic institution sport game Person practice sports and pastimes fidehell bran dubh taiplis hurling crosanacht cearrbhach Gaelic Athletic Association Tomas Laidir O Coisdealbha Dan Donnelly mumming Ohogain, Daithi

    The sports and pastimes of Ireland carried a heavy load of custom and story, and the storytellers dwelt fondly on those who shone in the different games. The old tales speak over and over of a board-game they call fidehell, which was near to what we know as chess, played with two armies of men pegged into place on a board, though the exact rules have not come down to us. Another game that turns up often is bran dubh, whose name means the black raven, and from the twelfth century onward the game of backgammon, taken from the French word for tables and called taiplis, was the delight of the upper ranks. Beyond skill in arms and in running and leaping, the chief pastime of the young men is shown as a form of hurling, in which great numbers on either side struck at the ball with a stick called the caman, each camp in turn guarding a hollow in the earth or a stake set upright with its two ends fixed in the soil, and the enemy sought to drive a ball of brass into that goal. The hunting of birds, of deer and of the wild pig is a constant business in the texts, and so is the running of horses in races.The evening leisure of the great houses turned on feasting and on the telling of stories, with ale and mead the favourite drinks. A schooling from the Middle Ages lists the kinds of tale the poets used to unfold: loves and banquets, journeys to the other world, great gatherings of armies, the ruin of houses, and the plundering of cattle. These narratives were the craft of the professional poets and of their reciters, yet other sorts of amusement were sought at feasts as well. The gleemen, commonly named crosdin, followed a thriving trade despite the clergy's sharp blame for the indecent turns of some of their shows; they were gifted jugglers and tumblers, and made a special study of a comic medley of verse and prose that came to be known after them as crosanacht, a practice the poets kept up into recent times, and there can be little question that the trickster air of the poet in later folk-memory stems from these medieval merrymakers. The common folk had their own humbler forms of good cheer, of which no clear record survives, but it may safely be assumed that the sorts of stories told among plain people in the Middle Ages were much the same in kind as those of later times, and that the written literature, though often fenced off for the higher ranks, drew plainly on the talk of the countryside.No great break in these habits came until the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when the spread of English sway brought new games in its train. Card-playing and the throwing of dice caught the Irish so quickly that many grew altogether enslaved to them. Writers of the Elizabethan age describe with horror how professional gamesters lurked beside the open road waiting for travellers, and how some wagered everything they owned, even to their clothes and a share of their own flesh, on a turn of the cards. The very name for such a gambler, cearrbhach, is kin to the word for a cut, which hints that these fellows liked the swift and deadly game of cutting the pack. The clergy of every creed hated gambling, and through their preaching a warning tale that had been adopted from abroad gained great favour first in Leinster and then across the other provinces. In it a dark stranger came into a card-room and sat down to play, and when a card slipped to the floor and one of the players bent to lift it he saw that where his foot ought to be sat a cloven hoof, and the stranger, being the devil himself, vanished in a blast of fire, done out of his bid to snatch the souls of the players as a gift; the whole story was meant to keep people from the cards, and people said the evil one was never far off while such frenzied gaming was afoot.Set against this, the wagering of cards for small stakes, a goose or a duck, won a lasting welcome in country Ireland, with the many versions of the five-card game most in favour; and it is curious that the oral stories, where they dealt with wagers, put cards in the place that chess had filled in the older tales. The seventeenth century brought nastier town pastimes in bull-baiting, dog-fighting and cock-fighting, while in the open country the landlords took a new delight in riding to hounds after the fox. These same landlords also turned their hand to hurling and shaped it into teams made up of their tenants playing on chosen ground, yet a wilder sort of the game held on, with whole parishes contending to push the ball over a ditch or a boundary fence. Out of that more ordered play grew both the game of hockey and the great national hurling that the Gaelic Athletic Association put on a firm footing in 1884; the same body took charge of the Irish forms of football and handball, which had been played after a simple fashion for centuries already. The folklore is full of famous hurlers, some so handy they could keep the ball aloft by repeated strokes while they took off their clothes and put them on once more, and others who could run about a great building and strike the ball from every side without once letting it fall. A common legend tells how some celebrated player was carried off by the fairies by night and scored for a team of the other world before being set back in his own bed.Wrestling and boxing grew popular from the seventeenth century on, and lore gathered round the great champions of the two arts. Several of these stories turn on an Irish fighter overcoming an English one. The men of Connacht told how Tomas Laidir O Coisdealbha, whom they call Strong Thomas Costello, threw down a giant English wrestler that had come to Sligo to take on all comers; the unlucky visitor broke his neck in the bout and died, and the winner, who was a mighty athlete too, was said on one occasion to have outrun a horse, a fancy that is told of fine local runners in other counties as well. Tomas was the very man on whom folk legend loved to feed, being lithe and comely and long remembered for the sad love he bore Una MacDermott, for whom he made a well-known love-song; but her rich parents would not permit the match, and the pair pined away and died of broken hearts. Their burial is on an islet of Lough Key in County Roscommon, and out of each grave a tree grew so that the two leaned together over the graveyard. The most renowned of all Irish boxers was Dan Donnelly of Dublin, from 1814 to 1819 the conqueror in three great fights against English challengers, which were really long and brutal affairs, though the folklore has Donnelly winning them with a few mighty blows. His greatest victory came on the soil of England, and it is said King George the Fourth himself congratulated the victor, calling him the finest man in Ireland; Donnelly replied that he was in truth the worst man in Ireland but the best in England, a retort that echoes down through the lore of nineteenth-century athletes as a plain sounding of national grievance behind the play.A stock line in the folktales divides the night into three equal thirds for a company of heroes, a third given to drink, a third to music, and a third to deep sleep. Music, song and dance have found an endless welcome in Ireland in recent centuries, though the old genres cannot claim great antiquity. Singing may be followed with some exactness: the earliest Irish songs were in truth chanted poems, of both learned and popular sort, moving on the rhythm of the words. Songs of labour, such as those raised by the ploughmen and the women at the spinning-wheel, stayed in use into the nineteenth century, as did the chanted keening for the dead, and all of these may reach back to very old Irish ways of singing. The forms never hardened, for though the metres changed across the centuries, verse in Irish was commonly meant to be sung; a true melody grew up by degrees towards the close of the Middle Ages under the pull of Latin, French and English models, and it is demonstrable that the most tender love-songs in the Irish tongue borrow their topics and pictures from the courtly conventions that spread over western Europe between the thirteenth and the fifteenth century. These songs treat love as a sickness only its fulfilment can cure, and dwell on fair settings and on the secrets and vows of lovers, and they became the favourite sort of song in Ireland, so that the later flood of international ballads made little headway there at first. With the growing strength of English in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, however, versions of English and Scottish ballads took root and in time grew very popular.The harp, called the cruit or the clairseach, is the instrument named most often in the early and medieval writing, though the books also speak of others whose nature is less certain, such as a stringed tiompan and a pipe called the feadan. Music was held to be a finer art, and many a story tells of masters who could play three magical strains, the goltraighe that made the hearers weep, the geantraighe that made them light-hearted, and the suantraighe that brought on sleep. The harp had all but vanished by the eighteenth century, giving way to the bagpipe that had long been the music of the common people; the violin, known as the fiddle, reached Ireland in the seventeenth century and was the favourite for dance-music until it was largely pushed aside in the nineteenth by the accordion, called the mileoidin or little melody. Two Irish words stand for dancing, damhsa and rince, and both are borrowed from English, which suggests that Irish traditional dance is not of native growth. The old accounts of the withy dance, the long dance, and the sword dance show that group and processional dancing once held sway, but in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the jig, the reel and the hornpipe came over from Scotland and England and drove the old forms out. In the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries travelling dancing-masters went the rounds teaching deportment to the young, and they spread shaped versions of the quadrille, out of which sprang the set-dances that have been beloved for several generations.There was no regular theatrical stage in Gaelic Ireland, but plays in English were being put on in the towns from the fourteenth century. A half-dramatic folk pastime called mumming came in from England and won great favour in much of Ulster and Leinster in recent centuries; at Christmas the local people, suitably dressed up, went from door to door and gave pieces made up of long set speeches and the acting that went with them, shared among a mixed company of saints, devils and famous persons. The plainer pastimes took in throwing the weight, skittles and bowls, and many kinds of divination game were eagerly sought, chiefly by girls wishing to learn whom they should marry. Riddles and tongue-twisters amused the family circle, and grown people delighted in tracing their pedigrees and in turning over old words and proverbs. But above everything else, the stock-in-trade of Irish tradition was storytelling, and the breadth and polish of the craft is the very subject of Ohogain's book.

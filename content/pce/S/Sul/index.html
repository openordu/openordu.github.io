    Sul

    Christopher Godwin 2026-08-27 05:26:47 about 4 minutes

    place Place group goddess Person text Sul Aquae Sulis Bath Minerva Brigit Sulis Suleviae Historia Brittonum

    Sul was a British goddess of healing. She was honored at Bath in southwest England, where hot springs bubble up at about a million gallons of a day. The Romans called the town Aquae Sulis, the waters of Sul. The Roman joined Sul with Minerva, so people often called her Sulis Minerva. The site became a place of commerce and healing. Visitors bathed in the steaming water and prayed for a cure. A bronze figure found in the temple shows Sul as a warrior maiden. More than 6,000 coins were drawn from the spring, most of them Roman. The name of Sul may mean sun and eye, which points to a sun goddess. She also appears in the plural as the Suleviae. Some scholars still looked for a male sun god or read the name as a misspelling of the word for pig. She is sometimes placed beside Brigit, a British war goddess, and the two were joined in the minds of the people. Koch names Sulis a healer goddess of the British Celts and retribution, venerated at the thermal waters of Aquae Sulis in the Romano-British period. She was at times linked with the Roman Minerva in her healing role. Celtic coinage thrown into the spring in the first century AD shows that the cult already stood before the Romans built on the site. Only in the Flavian era, from AD 69 to 138, did the bath and temple complex give the cult its public weight. Roman and native inscriptions, and curse tablets, call on the goddess. The native solar side of the cult stayed strong even under Roman influence. A Medusa head on the temple pediment is clearly Celtic in its carving. Koch ties the name to the Indo-European word for the sun, seen in Welsh haul, Old Welsh houl, and Breton heol. The same root gives a transferred meaning of eye, as in Old Irish suil. The pairing of water and the sun marks other curative springs across the Roman-Celtic world, joining the world below with a sky or sun cult in the work of healing. The link of eye and spring runs through the life of Saint Brigit, where she takes out her own eye and a spring rises from the ground. In Old Welsh the word licat means both eye and spring, and serves in the tale of a marvellous spring in the Historia Brittonum. Sulis thus holds together the healing power of water, the light of the sun, and the sight of the eye.

    black cat and broomstick

    The black cat and broomstick together make up the standard picture of a witch in the popular storybooks, yet that feared figure had little real part in Irish folk memory. Kevin Danaher opened his account of the subject with the recollection of an aged woman he came upon as a boy while spending a summer in County Clare. She made her home alone in a tiny cottage of one room, with a black cat lying by the hearth and a heather besom standing behind the door, and to the child she seemed pulled straight from his books of fairy tales. The woman had lost her teeth and wore a set that had once belonged to a neighbour named Big Jim, and she was quick to hurl a curse at anyone who crossed her, so the people around her kept her pleased with presents of milk, greens and fruit. She also knew the use of healing herbs and gave out her remedies freely to those who came to her with proper respect. Danaher pointed out that, although she had the look of the character from the stories, the witch who rode through the night sky on a broom handle with her cat settled behind her was not a figure the people of the place believed in. A woman of her sort might be counted a bean feasa, gifted in cures and in the reading of signs, or an old cailleach said to lift the cows' milk while wearing the shape of a hare, but nobody ever called her a witch. What stood between her and many women like her was the simple truth that the witch whom Britain and Europe hunted so cruelly had no true home in Ireland. From the middle ages down to the closing years of the eighteen hundreds the dread of sorcery raged across Britain and the continent, among the unlettered and the wise alike. It was widely held that people of both sexes and of every age joined a hidden fellowship that carried evil into the community, each member giving up his soul to the devil in exchange for supernatural power. Entry into the company was marked by foul ceremonies that turned the Christian rite to mockery, and each member received a familiar spirit, a demon that wore the form of an animal, very often a black cat, or the figure of a misshapen man. Armed with such a helper the witch could call up gales, spoil the corn, sicken the herds, shift an illness from one sufferer to another, and ruin or destroy a foe by shaping his likeness and burning it or sticking it with pins. Rubbing a broom handle with a special ointment, the tales said, let the witch race through the air to the assembly. The great men of church and state took the danger in deadly earnest, and scholars produced books telling the public how to recognise a sorceress and how to stamp out the evil. Alarm carried across nearly every land of Europe, where many hundreds of accused persons were seized, put to the question, tried and killed, while frightened mobs drove countless others from their homes. Some of the accused truly deserved their fate, some were simply deluded, but the greater number were surely innocent. The parliaments passed stern statutes against sorcery; in England they were enacted in the reign of Henry the Eighth, and James the Sixth of Scotland, later king of England as well, gave the hunt his close attention and wrote a book denouncing it. In the time of the Commonwealth rewards tempted men to turn in witches, and schemers seized the chance to ruin their enemies with worthless charges. The sharpest of the witch finders was Matthew Hopkins of England, who grew rich on the trade, pricking suspects with needles to locate the mark of the beast, forcing them to rattle off prayers without a slip, and casting them into water to learn whether they would sink or float. When Hopkins himself failed the very trials he used on others it put an end to his calling, and he was at length accused and executed. Excess of this kind sobered the public mind, the panic slowly died, and in 1736 the English lawmakers finally repealed the witchcraft acts. Ireland, by contrast, witnessed only a handful of such hunts, and those few show how close the country came to sharing the horror. The best known was the affair of Dame Alice of Kilkenny in 1324, pursued by the bishop of Ossory, Richard Ledrede, an English-born churchman who had taken the see in 1317. She stood accused of casting aside the faith, of offering black cocks and other beasts to the devil at the meeting of crossroads, of doing away with three husbands by magic, and of keeping a familiar spirit called Robin that could show itself as a cat, as a black dog, or as a swarthy man. Folk said too that she swept the street outside her son's door while chanting a verse meant to draw the whole riches of the town into his house. The bishop ordered Dame Alice taken, but her influential friends kept the law at bay, and she slipped off to the capital and later crossed the sea to England, escaping the hunt for good. Her allies were not so fortunate; some, after confessing under torture, were burned at the stake. This trial stands alone as the one known case of witchcraft before the Reformation, and that freedom was not a trick of lost records, for the parliament at Trim in 1447 assured the king that sorcery was unknown in the island. When a later hunt arrived in Kilkenny in 1578, the judges found the jails so crowded that they set to work at once and condemned thirty-six people, among them those who had been held.
